The Mechanism

Take a fair share of rent. Retire debt with it.

Gas companies pay a modest royalty and a repaired rent tax. Treasury splits the net new receipts between direct debt retirement and a resource tranche run by the Future Fund Board. The tranche's distributions also retire debt.

01
⛽ Gas Companies
Three changes, each aimed at rent rather than revenue. First, an 8% royalty on LNG export value that pays no royalty today, phased in over four years for existing projects, with any state royalty credited so nothing is charged twice. Second, the PRRT stays: the LNG deduction cap tightens from 90% towards 60%, and related-party gas is valued at a benchmark netback. Third, new projects may elect a Norway-style cash-flow rent tax (immediate expensing, refundable losses) so the tax does not deter investment that is profitable before tax. Domestic sales under the proposed 20% reservation scheme sit outside the royalty.
Royalty floor + repaired rent tax, grandfathered
Net receipts, after the company-tax deduction
02
🏛 Government / Treasury
Collects the receipts as ordinary revenue and credits the net new amount to two ring-fenced accounts under a legislated split (50/50 by default). The Parliamentary Budget Office reports each year on whether the deficit path improved by the amount credited. Money is fungible, so the rule only works if it is checked.
The split is a dial: debt-first or fund-first
Share to debt: retire maturing securities at about 4.8%
03
📉 Debt Reduction Account
Applied only to retiring maturing Commonwealth securities instead of refinancing them. Each dollar retired saves the Budget's assumed 4.8% cost of new Treasury Bond issuance, a certain return with no market risk. The interest saved is recycled into further retirements.
Risk-free return: about 4.8% a year
Share to the fund: distributions flow back to debt from year 5
04
🌏 Resource Tranche (Future Fund Board)
Managed by the existing Future Fund Board under a separate mandate and reported separately, so no new agency is built. The Future Fund holds about $267B and targets CPI + 4 to 5%. From year 5, 3% of the tranche's value each year is paid to the Debt Reduction Account. The fund's returns help pay down debt while the principal keeps compounding.
Expected 7% in the model, with market risk
Design Choices

Seven hard questions, and how the design answers them.

An earlier draft of this page proposed a flat 25% levy on export revenue and a bond issue to super funds. Both had serious weaknesses. This version is built to survive a Treasury review.

⚖️
1. Tax rent, not revenue
A flat 25% of export value taxes a project's revenue whether or not it earns a return, which deters marginal and undeveloped fields. Here the royalty stays small (an owner's charge for the resource), a 40% rent tax catches profit above a normal return, and new projects can elect a cash-flow version. Norway's tax is neutral for the same reason: investment is deducted immediately.
🛡
2. Grandfather the past, give certainty for the future
Existing projects keep their accrued PRRT deductions. The royalty phases in over four years (2, 4, 6, then 8%). The rate schedule is legislated for 15 years, and any change requires an independent review and five years' notice. That cannot bind a future Parliament, but it raises the cost of changing the deal, which is what lenders price.
🔧
3. Repair the PRRT, do not abolish it
The government's own position is that most LNG projects will not pay significant PRRT until the 2030s, so abolishing the tax would give up that revenue. The general uplift is the long-term bond rate plus 5%, and the 2023 cap (deductions limited to 90% of assessable receipts) already brings some payments forward. The Australia Institute estimates a 60% cap would raise roughly $2B a year more than the 90% cap.
📉
4. Debt first, then a fund
A fund earning an expected 7% while the Commonwealth borrows at about 4.8% is partly borrowing to invest. So half of net receipts retire debt directly at a certain 4.8%, and the other half goes to a fund whose distributions also retire debt. The benefit is measured as net debt reduction, which counts both.
🏛
5. Use the existing institution, drop the bond leg
The earlier draft had a Commonwealth-guaranteed bond sold to super funds. It added a contingent liability and complexity for roughly $0.5B a year of net carry, so it is gone. The fund tranche is run by the Future Fund Board, which already exists, has a mandate and holds about $267B.
🔍
6. Be honest about fungibility
If receipts are credited to a debt account but spending rises elsewhere, debt does not fall. The rule only works if it is tied to a published net debt path and checked by the Parliamentary Budget Office every year.
⛽
7. Do not stack on domestic gas policy
The proposed 20% east coast reservation scheme (from 1 July 2027, not yet legislated) is meant to lower domestic prices. The royalty applies to export sales only, so reserved volumes are not taxed twice.

The Fiscal Rule

Debt first.
Dividends later.

Net receipts are credited to two accounts under a legislated split. Both exist to reduce net debt. Only after net debt reaches a legislated anchor does Parliament get to allocate fund distributions elsewhere.

Debt Reduction Account
Retire maturing securities
Balance can be used only to retire maturing Commonwealth securities rather than refinance them. Interest saved is added back and used the same way.
Governed by primary legislation, reported on by the PBO each year
Resource Tranche
Invested by the Future Fund Board
A separate mandate and separate reporting. From year 5, 3% of the tranche's value a year is paid to the Debt Reduction Account. Principal is not drawn.
After the net debt anchor is met, Parliament may allocate distributions through the budget
Where net receipts go, per year at full phase-in
The split is a slider in the projection above. At 100% to debt this is the lowest-risk version.
Why the split exists
Retiring debt earns a certain 4.8%, the Budget's assumed cost of new borrowing. A diversified fund is expected to earn more, but only by taking market risk, and the Commonwealth is already a large borrower. Splitting the receipts keeps most of the certainty while still building an asset for later generations. A legislated anchor, such as a net debt target set by Parliament, is what decides when the fund's distributions can be spent rather than used to retire debt.

Two precedents, one lesson.

The Critical Design Decision
Both precedents work because the money has a clear purpose and a neutral mandate. Norway's tax is also neutral toward investment, because costs are deducted immediately. The AFRF borrows that tax logic for new projects and the Future Fund's institution for the tranche. It does not copy Norway's 78% rate, which applies to net profit and is a different instrument from a royalty on export value.
Guardrails

Governance principles.

The design only works if the money actually goes where the rule says. Five principles are proposed.

01
Existing institution
The resource tranche is managed by the Future Fund Board under a separate mandate and reported separately. Investment decisions stay with the Board, as they do for the Future Fund today.
02
Ring-fenced debt account
The Debt Reduction Account can only be used to retire maturing Commonwealth securities. Changing that requires an Act of Parliament, not a ministerial instrument.
03
Independent check
The Parliamentary Budget Office publishes an annual statement: receipts credited, debt retired, fund distributions, and whether the deficit and debt path improved by the same amount.
04
Legislated schedule and review
The royalty schedule, grandfathering rules and PRRT cap are set in primary legislation. An independent review every five years, with five years' notice before any rate change, gives lenders a stable planning basis.
05
Net debt anchor
Parliament sets a net debt anchor. Until it is met, all distributions retire debt. After it is met, Parliament may allocate distributions through the normal budget process.
Stress Test

Five hard challenges. Five direct answers.

These come from industry, from Treasury and from economists. Each answer says where the design holds and where it does not. Tap a challenge to see it.

Challenge
"A new royalty on top of the PRRT and company tax will make Scarborough and Browse uneconomic."
▾
The answer
The royalty is small and deductible. At 8% of export value and deductible against company tax, the after-tax cost to a company paying 30% tax is about 5.6% of revenue. New projects can also elect a cash-flow rent tax with immediate expensing, which does not discourage projects that are profitable before tax.

The honest limit. A royalty is owed whether or not a year is profitable, so it bites marginal and undeveloped fields first. Browse in particular should be modelled project by project before anyone claims it stays economic.

Sunk projects are grandfathered. Existing projects keep accrued PRRT deductions and the royalty phases in over four years (2, 4, 6, then 8%). Scarborough was 67% complete in December 2025, so its main investment decisions predate any change.
Royalty after company tax: about 5.6% of export value.
Phase-in: 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%.
Challenge
"States already charge royalties, so a federal royalty is unworkable."
▾
The answer
Credit, do not stack. Any state royalty paid is credited against the federal royalty up to 8%, so Queensland's regime is untouched and nothing is charged twice. The federal royalty fills the gap where nothing is charged today.

The inconsistency is the real problem. Queensland collected $1.69B in royalties in 2024-25, including on gas exported as LNG, while the federal government collected $1.42B in PRRT from the larger offshore areas it controls. The Australia Institute estimates more than half of LNG exports pay no royalty.

Legal basis. Section 90 gives the Commonwealth exclusive power over duties of customs and excise. Whether a court would treat a royalty on a Commonwealth-waters resource as the owner's charge, or a levy on exports as a duty, is a question for constitutional lawyers and shapes the drafting.

Challenge
"Putting money in a fund while the Commonwealth borrows is just borrowing to invest."
▾
The answer
Partly right, which is why half of receipts go straight to retiring debt. Retiring a maturing bond saves the Budget's assumed 4.8% cost of new borrowing with no market risk. The fund tranche is expected to earn more (7% in the model), but that is payment for risk, and over a bad decade it can earn less than the debt costs.

Challenge
"This is a rounding error against a trillion dollars of debt."
▾
The answer
On scale, yes. About $3.2B a year in net receipts is roughly 0.10% of GDP, against $1,051B of securities on issue and net debt of $617B in 2026-27. In the base case, 20 years of receipts, recycled interest and fund returns improve net debt by roughly $88B in nominal terms, about 8% of today's gross debt.

What it does well. It captures a larger share of the rent on a public resource that is currently under-collected, it is permanent, and it ties the proceeds to a measurable debt outcome. It is a contribution, not a solution. The larger levers for debt are the budget balance and economic growth.
Base case, year 20: net debt about $88B better.
Interest saved in year 20: about $2.3B.
Challenge
"The industry already pays $21.9 billion in taxes and royalties, so it is paying its fair share."
▾
The answer
That figure includes ordinary company income tax, and that tax is now being paid: Chevron Australia's ATO-reported 2023-24 income tax payable was about $3.5B, and the ATO told a 2026 Senate inquiry that gas exporters paid over $24B of company tax and about $6B of PRRT in the four years to 2023-24. The question is the resource-specific return, meaning PRRT and royalties.

That return has been small. PRRT was $1.42B in 2024-25 against LNG exports of roughly $65B. The government's own position is that most LNG projects will not pay significant PRRT until the 2030s, and the 2026 Budget still had PRRT falling during a gas price boom.

Importers tax this gas too. The Australia Institute found Japan's energy import tax raised about $8.9B on gas imports from all sources over five years, against about $7.0B from Australia's PRRT (which covers all petroleum). On Australian LNG alone Japan's take is smaller, an estimated $0.7B a year, and the comparison is disputed. Treat it as an illustration, not a headline.
