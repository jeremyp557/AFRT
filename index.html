The Mechanism

Three parties. Clean roles.

Government collects the full levy on day one. The Fund invests it. Superannuation funds buy a small, Commonwealth-guaranteed bond issue. No private credit arrangements, no politically loaded mandates.

01
⛽ Gas Companies
The PRRT is abolished. In its place: a 25% levy on LNG export value, assessed against customs data the ATO already holds. Volume and price are recorded on every shipment, which makes the base far harder to shift than profit. It is not manipulation-proof: for sales to related parties, value is set at the higher of the contract price and an independent benchmark netback. A relief floor caps the levy at 80% of audited project-level EBITDA in a genuinely distressed year, using a tightly defined, ATO-audited measure with no related-party charges or intercompany interest.

02
🏛 Government / Treasury
Receives the full levy in cash. Deposits receipts into the Australia Future Resource Fund under a legislated schedule. The Fund operates off-balance-sheet, consistent with the existing Future Fund treatment. No deferral, no compromise on revenue.

03
🌏 Australia Future Resource Fund
An independent sovereign vehicle modelled on the Future Fund Act. Invests on a mandate of maximising risk-adjusted returns for current and future Australians. No sectoral, political or ideological investment constraints — asset allocation decisions are made by the Board on return merit alone. May issue a limited volume of Commonwealth-guaranteed long-dated bonds to institutional investors, which adds modest leverage to the portfolio.
Return mandate — not a policy mandate
Commonwealth-guaranteed long bonds, priced as a small spread over ACGBs (about 5.5–6%)
04
🏦 Superannuation Funds
Buy Commonwealth-guaranteed AFRF bonds priced off the long end of the government curve. 15 to 20 year ACGBs yielded roughly 5.6–5.7% at the end of September 2026, so a new-issue premium of a few tenths of a percent puts the target at about 5.5–6%. The attraction for trustees is more long-dated, AAA-quality supply, not a yield above government bonds. The bonds are a distribution channel to members, not the main source of the Fund's wealth.

🗑
PRRT Abolished
The Petroleum Resource Rent Tax — with its cost uplift provisions, intercompany debt offsets, and transfer pricing shelters — is replaced entirely. One rate. One base. Assessed against customs data the ATO already holds, with a benchmark for related-party sales. Far less room for deduction planning than a profits tax, though not none.
🛟
The Earnings Floor
The levy is capped at 80% of project-level EBITDA in a distressed year. EBITDA is a company-reported number, so it is the one place manipulation could re-enter. Safeguards: the measure is defined in legislation, excludes related-party charges and intercompany interest, is ATO-audited, and is trued up after the fact. This reduces the risk. It does not remove it.
👁
The Honest Trade-Off
Removing the punitive intent argument also removes the gas lobby's best legal weapon. The AFRF's neutral mandate makes the levy harder to challenge in court and harder to repeal at the next election. Gas companies get certainty and a floor — but the levy is more permanent, not less. That is the honest trade.
🔮
20-Year Planning Clarity
A fixed rate with a defined floor lets gas companies model the obligation more accurately across the full life of a project. Scarborough, Barossa, Papua LNG — all can be financed with known tax parameters rather than a profits tax whose effective rate depends on year-end accounting outcomes. A gross levy is not neutral the way Norway's cash-flow tax is, so undeveloped fields (Browse, for example) need to be modelled against it before anyone claims they stay economic.

The Fiscal Rule

The Fund stays intact.
The dividend does the work.

The Fund's investment mandate is never touched. Once it reaches sufficient scale, a separate piece of legislation — the fiscal rule — releases up to 3% of the Fund's value annually into a dedicated national account. Parliament allocates that account. The Fund keeps compounding.

Why the separation matters
Embedding spending allocations inside the Fund's mandate would compromise its independence and hand critics the argument that the Fund is a political instrument. The fiscal rule keeps the two completely separate — the same design Norway has used since 2001. The Fund remains commercially credible to super fund trustees. The dividend remains democratically accountable to Parliament. Neither contaminates the other.
The Precedent

Norway did this. It worked.

Norway established its petroleum fund in 1990 and has run it with a simple mandate: maximise long-term returns for current and future Norwegians. No policy agenda, no ideological constraints. Its market value series starts in 1996, and at June 2026 it stood at NOK 22.7 trillion, the largest sovereign wealth fund in the world.

The Critical Design Decision
Norway's fund is durable because its mandate contains no policy agenda. It was not established to achieve any environmental, industrial or social outcome. It was established to convert finite petroleum wealth into permanent financial wealth. That single principle has held across many changes of government. The AFRF adopts the institutional design, not Norway's tax design: Norway taxes net profit with investment deducted immediately, whereas the AFRF levy is on export value.
Independence

Governance principles.

The Fund's independence from political direction is essential to its credibility with institutional investors and to its legal durability. Five foundational principles are proposed.

01
Legislative Independence
The Board is appointed by the Treasurer but cannot be directed by the Treasurer. Board decisions on asset allocation are final and not subject to ministerial approval. Modelled directly on the Future Fund Act 2006.
02
Mandate Stability
The mandate is defined in primary legislation and can only be amended by Act of Parliament. Ministerial instruments cannot modify the investment mandate. The mandate survives changes of government.
03
Full Transparency
The Fund publishes quarterly holdings data, annual performance reports, and an independently audited statement of compliance with the mandate. Every dollar is publicly accountable.
04
Non-Interference Principle
The Fund may not hold a controlling interest in any Australian company. It invests as a financial participant, not a strategic one. This prevents the Fund from becoming a vehicle for industrial policy.
05
Member Returns
Returns from AFRF bonds are distributed to superannuation members through their existing fund structure, with no government intermediation of individual returns. The mechanism is distribution through markets, not through government.
Responding to the Lobby

Four claims. Four direct answers.

The gas industry has a well-rehearsed set of objections. Each one has a direct counter grounded in data. Tap each claim to see the response.

Lobby claim
"Jurisdictional complexity makes a federal levy unworkable — states and territories already have their own royalty regimes."
▾
The answer
The jurisdictional argument collapses under scrutiny — because the complexity it cites is itself evidence of failure, not a barrier to reform.

The AFRF levy is a federal export levy. Section 90 of the Constitution gives the Commonwealth exclusive power over duties of customs and excise. A levy assessed at the point of export, against customs data the ATO already holds, is designed to sit within Commonwealth jurisdiction. Whether a court would characterise it that way, or as an income-type tax, is a question for constitutional lawyers and shapes how the legislation is drafted.

State royalties remain a separate, parallel instrument. The levy operates at the export border; state royalties operate at the wellhead. The two instruments don't interact. States keep whatever royalties they currently collect.

The inconsistency is the federal problem. Queensland collected $1.69B in petroleum royalties in 2024-25, including on gas exported as LNG, while the federal government collected $1.42B in PRRT from the much larger offshore areas it controls. The Australia Institute estimates more than half of Australia's LNG exports pay no royalty at all.
2024-25: Queensland royalties $1.69B vs federal PRRT $1.42B.
More than half of LNG exports pay no royalty (Australia Institute).
Lobby claim
"The industry already pays $21.9 billion in taxes and royalties — it's paying its fair share."
▾
The answer
The industry's own figure includes ordinary company income tax that any business would pay, and that tax is now being paid: Chevron Australia's ATO-reported 2023-24 income tax payable was about $3.5B, and the ATO told a 2026 Senate inquiry that gas exporters paid over $24B of company tax and about $6B of PRRT in the four years to 2023-24. The question is the resource-specific return, meaning PRRT and royalties, which is where the shortfall sits.

The PRRT has delivered very little, by design. Receipts were $1.42B in 2024-25 against LNG exports of roughly $65B. Treasury's own 2023 position was that most LNG projects would not pay significant PRRT until the 2030s, and the 2026 Budget still had PRRT falling during a gas price boom.

Norway shows high take and investment can coexist. Norway's marginal rate on petroleum profit is 78%, but it is a cash-flow tax with investment deducted immediately, which is why it is investment-neutral. A 25% levy on export value is a different instrument and is not neutral, so the comparison supports the principle of a high resource take, not the specific rate.

Importers tax this gas too. The Australia Institute found Japan's energy import tax raised about $8.9B on gas imports from all sources over five years, against about $7.0B from Australia's PRRT (which covers all petroleum, not just gas). On Australian LNG specifically, Japan's take is smaller, an estimated $0.7B a year, and the comparison is disputed. Treat it as an illustration of the principle, not a headline.
PRRT 2024-25: $1.42B.
LNG export earnings 2024-25: about $65B (REQ).
Lobby claim
"A 25% levy will kill future investment — projects like Scarborough and Browse will become uneconomic."
▾
The answer
Three points dismantle this argument directly.

First, the floor limits the downside in bad years. The 80% EBITDA cap bounds the levy in a price crash, so a single bad year cannot push a project into loss because of the levy. It does not make every project economic: a gross levy still takes 25% of revenue in normal years, and marginal or undeveloped fields need project-level modelling.

Second, high resource taxes and investment can coexist. Norway has taxed petroleum at a 78% marginal rate for decades and still attracts heavy investment, but its regime deducts investment immediately, so it does not discourage projects that are profitable before tax. That design feature is the real lesson, and the AFRF should consider an equivalent treatment for new capital.

Third, certainty has value. A fixed, predictable levy is easier to model than a profits tax whose effective rate is uncertain until year-end, which can help bankability. Lenders will still price in the risk that a government raises a rate once it has one.
Scarborough: 67% complete as of December 2025.
Investment already committed before any levy change.
Lobby claim
"Australia is different to Norway — you can't compare our geology, market structure, or sovereign risk profile."
▾
The answer
The Norway comparison is not a geological argument — it is an institutional one. The relevant precedent is not that Norway has oil and Australia has LNG. The relevant precedent is that a resource-producing nation designed an independent sovereign fund with a neutral returns mandate, funded it through a resource levy, and created permanent national wealth that outlasts the resource.

The AFRF adopts the institutional design, not the geology. The Future Fund Act already exists as an Australian precedent for exactly this kind of independent vehicle. The AFRF is an extension of a model Australia has already proved it can run.

The "sovereign risk" argument runs both ways. The claim that Australia cannot raise a resource levy without damaging its sovereign risk profile treats the current arrangement as the baseline investors are entitled to. LNG exports earned roughly $191B across 2023-24 to 2025-26 (REQ) while PRRT averaged about $1.4B a year. The federal government has also so far ruled out a gas export tax (May 2026 Budget), so the sovereign risk argument is as much about political credibility as geology.
LNG exports, 2023-24 to 2025-26: about $191B (REQ).
PRRT over five years: about $7B (Australia Institute).
