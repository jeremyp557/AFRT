The Mechanism

Three parties. Clean roles.

Government collects the full levy on day one. The Fund invests it. Superannuation co-invests alongside. No private credit arrangements, no compounding debt, no politically loaded mandates.

PRRT Abolished
The Petroleum Resource Rent Tax — with its cost uplift provisions, intercompany debt offsets, and transfer pricing shelters — is replaced entirely. One rate. One base. Assessed against customs data the ATO already holds. The compliance industry built around PRRT minimisation has no role in this system.
🛟
The Earnings Floor
The levy is capped at 80% of EBITDA in any financial year. This circuit breaker only activates when global LNG prices collapse to the point where a pure revenue levy would exceed actual profit. In normal years it is irrelevant. In a genuinely distressed year it prevents confiscation — and removes the industry's only legitimate grievance.
👁
The Honest Trade-Off
Removing the punitive intent argument also removes the gas lobby's best legal weapon. The AFRF's neutral mandate makes the levy harder to challenge in court and harder to repeal at the next election. Gas companies get certainty and a floor — but the levy is more permanent, not less. That is the honest trade.
🔮
20-Year Planning Clarity
A fixed revenue rate with a defined floor allows gas companies to model the obligation accurately across the full life of a project. Scarborough, Barossa, Papua LNG — all can be financed with known tax parameters rather than a profits tax whose effective rate depends on year-end accounting outcomes.

The Fiscal Rule

The Fund stays intact.
The dividend does the work.

The Fund's investment mandate is never touched. Once it reaches sufficient scale, a separate piece of legislation — the fiscal rule — releases up to 3% of the Fund's value annually into a dedicated national account. Parliament allocates that account. The Fund keeps compounding.

Why the separation matters
Embedding spending allocations inside the Fund's mandate would compromise its independence and hand critics the argument that the Fund is a political instrument. The fiscal rule keeps the two completely separate — the same design Norway has used since 2001. The Fund remains commercially credible to super fund trustees. The dividend remains democratically accountable to Parliament. Neither contaminates the other.

The Critical Design Decision
Norway's fund is durable because its mandate contains no policy agenda. It was not established to achieve any environmental, industrial or social outcome. It was established to convert finite petroleum wealth into permanent financial wealth. That single principle has survived eleven changes of government. The AFRF adopts it directly.
Independence

Governance principles.

The Fund's independence from political direction is essential to its credibility with institutional investors and to its legal durability. Five foundational principles are proposed.

01
Legislative Independence
The Board is appointed by the Treasurer but cannot be directed by the Treasurer. Board decisions on asset allocation are final and not subject to ministerial approval. Modelled directly on the Future Fund Act 2006.
02
Mandate Stability
The mandate is defined in primary legislation and can only be amended by Act of Parliament. Ministerial instruments cannot modify the investment mandate. The mandate survives changes of government.
03
Full Transparency
The Fund publishes quarterly holdings data, annual performance reports, and an independently audited statement of compliance with the mandate. Every dollar is publicly accountable.
04
Non-Interference Principle
The Fund may not hold a controlling interest in any Australian company. It invests as a financial participant, not a strategic one. This prevents the Fund from becoming a vehicle for industrial policy.
05
Member Returns
Returns from AFRF bonds are distributed to superannuation members through their existing fund structure, with no government intermediation of individual returns. The mechanism is distribution through markets, not through government.
Responding to the Lobby

Four claims. Four direct answers.

The gas industry has a well-rehearsed set of objections. Each one has a direct counter grounded in data. Tap each claim to see the response.

Lobby claim
"Jurisdictional complexity makes a federal levy unworkable — states and territories already have their own royalty regimes."
▾
The answer
The jurisdictional argument collapses under scrutiny — because the complexity it cites is itself evidence of failure, not a barrier to reform.

The AFRF levy is a federal export levy. Under Section 90 of the Constitution, the Commonwealth has exclusive power over export duties. A levy assessed at the point of export — against customs data the ATO already holds — sits entirely within Commonwealth jurisdiction. No state royalty regime intersects with it.

State royalties remain a separate, parallel instrument. The levy operates at the export border; state royalties operate at the wellhead. The two instruments don't interact. States keep whatever royalties they currently collect.

The complexity the lobby describes is actually a federal failure. Six of Australia's ten LNG export facilities pay zero royalties — state or federal — because they extract gas from Commonwealth waters and the federal government has simply never charged for it. The lobby is asking Australia not to fix a problem they created.
56% of Australian LNG exports attract zero royalties — state or federal.
Most of this gas is extracted from Commonwealth waters.
Lobby claim
"The industry already pays $21.9 billion in taxes and royalties — it's paying its fair share."
▾
The answer
The $21.9 billion figure includes ordinary company income tax that any business in any sector would pay. Stripping that out, the resource-specific return to Australia — the PRRT and royalties that reflect the value of the resource itself — is a fraction of that figure.

The PRRT has been called a systemic failure by the ATO itself. In 2020-21, Woodside, ExxonMobil, Shell, Chevron, INPEX and APLNG paid no tax at all on a collective $34 billion of income. In 2021-22, when record prices lifted their combined income to $56.3 billion, they paid just $454 million in total.

The comparison is Norway. Norway applies a 78% effective tax rate to petroleum profits. Its industry is thriving, still attracting investment, and still expanding. If 78% is compatible with a healthy industry, the claim that 25% of export revenue is existential is not credible.

Japan collected more from Australian gas than Australia did. Japan collected nearly $40 billion in taxes on Australian gas and coal imports over the past five years. Australia collected $7 billion from the PRRT over the same period on the same gas.
$34B combined income in 2020-21.
Tax paid: $0 across six of the largest operators.
Lobby claim
"A 25% levy will kill future investment — projects like Scarborough and Browse will become uneconomic."
▾
The answer
Three points dismantle this argument directly.

First, the earnings floor removes the genuine risk. The 80% EBITDA cap means that in any year where the levy would genuinely exceed profit — a price crash scenario — the obligation is automatically limited. Projects cannot be made loss-making by the levy. The "uneconomic" scenario the lobby describes cannot occur under the AFRF design.

Second, Norway invalidates the principle. Norway has taxed petroleum at 78% for decades while simultaneously being one of the most active offshore exploration environments in the world. Chevron, Shell, Equinor and others continue to invest there heavily. High resource taxes and investment are demonstrably compatible.

Third, certainty has value. The gas lobby's own project finance teams know that a fixed, predictable, manipulation-proof levy is easier to model and finance than a profits tax whose effective rate is uncertain until year-end. Replacing the PRRT with a transparent revenue levy actually improves the bankability of long-life projects.
Scarborough: 67% complete as of December 2025.
Investment already committed before any levy change.
Lobby claim
"Australia is different to Norway — you can't compare our geology, market structure, or sovereign risk profile."
▾
The answer
The Norway comparison is not a geological argument — it is an institutional one. The relevant precedent is not that Norway has oil and Australia has LNG. The relevant precedent is that a resource-producing nation designed an independent sovereign fund with a neutral returns mandate, funded it through a resource levy, and created permanent national wealth that outlasts the resource.

The AFRF adopts the institutional design, not the geology. The Future Fund Act already exists as an Australian precedent for exactly this kind of independent vehicle. The AFRF is an extension of a model Australia has already proved it can run.

The "sovereign risk" argument runs both ways. The claim that Australia cannot raise a resource levy without damaging its sovereign risk profile implies that the current arrangement — where $149 billion in gas was exported over four years with minimal resource-specific return — is somehow the baseline investors expect to be protected. It is not. It is an anomaly in global terms, not a norm.
